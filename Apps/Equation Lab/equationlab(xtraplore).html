Field guide
      
Fourteen genres

      
Escape repeats a map and colours each point by how quickly it runs away. Attractor follows one chaotic orbit for millions of steps. Newton basin walks every pixel toward a root of a complex function. Domain colour paints a complex function directly, hue for angle and bands for size. Flow field releases particles into a velocity field. Lattice evaluates a formula on whole-number cells and wraps it by a modulus. Automaton grows a one-dimensional world row by row. Contour draws level bands of a height field. Harmonograph traces decaying pendulums. Quantum field speaks FXplore’s operator tongue over the plane. Supershape lights a parametric surface in 3D, grown from the superformula Shape Atlas uses, or from a BIOCODEX genome. Current and Pulse are living genres: their equations take a time t, and ▶ Play animates them on the main thread while the still render and the C source freeze t = 0. Play also works on any flow field. Tongue lets a civilization speak: its rhythm, its sentence pulses and the whole census become operators, and the plane is tiled with one colour per letter of its alphabet.

FXplore stations

      
The FXplore langpack “Felix” is embedded byte for byte: four glyph sets and about three thousand station grammars, each keyed by the frequency it was heard on. A grammar is its seeds, word length, punctuation weights and syllable pattern. Tune to a station and every mint is re-spoken in its dialect: the syllable pattern chooses which generation replaces a plain function, the seeds bend the coefficients, the punctuation decides how eager it is. Frequencies past 10⁹ are deep space, where the dial ran away. All of it is procedural, synthetic data, labelled as such. Import another .fweddle langpack to add its stations.

      
Images

      
Drop, paste or choose any image. The lab keeps a small brightness map of it, up to 128 cells across, and equations read it with img(x, y). The image repeats every whole unit, so it tiles without end. Mint from image weaves it into a new equation, and Use its colours builds a palette from it. Exports embed the image so the equation stays reproducible.

What “1 in 10ⁿ” means

      
Each generator is a grammar. Every choice it makes, which term, which function, each coefficient to three or four decimal places, multiplies the number of equations it could have produced. The lab counts those choices as it goes and shows the total. At 10²⁰ and beyond, the chance that anyone has written down the same equation is negligible. Small totals are an honest warning: a short rule can coincide with one already in a book, so treat the number as odds, not proof.

      
Reading the picture

      
Rose pixels mean the arithmetic broke down there, usually a division by zero or an overflow. Dark pixels in escape and basin renders did not settle within the step limit; that does not prove they never would. Density genres refine as they render, so the first frames are faint.

      
Writing your own

      
Use * to multiply and ^ for powers. Real genres know abs, sin, cos, tan, atan, atan2, sinh, cosh, tanh, sqrt, log, exp, pow, floor, ceil, min, max, sign, mod, fract, hypot, and, or, xor, plus pi and e. Complex genres know sin, cos, tan, sinh, cosh, tanh, exp, log, sqrt, conj, abs, re, im, arg and the constant i. The sliders a, b, p and q light up once your equation uses them.

      
Xtraplore

      
Below the picture, eleven instruments animate the active equation together with a synthetic civilization. Rosetta transcribes the equation into the civilization’s script as it is generated. Cadence plays its word-length rhythm as a waveform with the equation riding on it. Scriptorium writes a manuscript, choosing every letter by reading the picture. Lexicon draws the alphabet stroke by stroke. Census shows every civilization at once and lets you tap one. Ether is a procedural waterfall. Congress puts the saved civilizations in session. Genesis grows an automaton seeded with the grammar. Atlas maps the saved civilizations as territories whose borders drift with the equation. Orrery orbits the active civilization’s channel neighbours around it, orbits bent by the equation. Choir sings three grammars as a score, pitched by the equation; Sound turns on a small synthesiser for Choir and Cadence. Keys 1 to 9 and 0 switch instruments, X plays and pauses. Any instrument can be recorded as a GIF or saved as a PNG frame; the recording steps time at a fixed rate, so it is smooth whatever the device was doing.

      
Civilizations and the langpack

      
The civilizations come from an FXplore langpack embedded in this file: thousands of grammars, each captured from one of FXplore’s own procedural channels, with the word-length rhythm, sentence length, punctuation weights and transmission count it logged, plus four glyph sets with FXplore’s notes on them. Everything is simulated; the lab labels it so and never claims otherwise. Each grammar is given a name, an English reading of its numbers and a synthetic alphabet with numerals and operator marks, all generated here from its channel number, so the same civilization looks the same on every visit. Old Italic and the Voynich EVA stand-ins are drawn in the lab’s own hand, the second as an invented imitation, because the manuscript’s script has no encoding. Six of the saved grammars also carry FXplore’s own dossier, a label, a kind such as wyrmline or calculus choir, a head glyph, an alphabet and a sample line, all shown verbatim in the Langpack panel; choose “FXplore’s repertoire” under Written in to have such a civilization write with those glyphs. Tuning a station in the Stations panel makes that station the speaking civilization until one is pinned. Open another langpack in the Langpack panel, or download the embedded capsule.

      
The civilization words

      
Four operators let any real equation speak in the active civilization’s cadence: tongue(x) is its rhythm as a waveform, pulse(x) its sentence marks, census(x) how much was heard from every civilization by rank, and civmap(u, v) the whole census as a texture. Change the civilization and the equation changes with it; exports remember which civilization was speaking, and the fingerprint includes it. C export does not cover these words yet.
